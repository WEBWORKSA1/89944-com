Margin vs Markup: The Pricing Mistake That Quietly Kills Profit

Clear definitions, formulas, a conversion table and real examples showing why a 40% markup is only a 28.6% margin.

By 89944 Price Lab Editorial · Updated November 2026

  2. The trap
  3. Quick conversion table
  4. Get the cost right first
  5. Which one should your team use?
  6. Typical gross margins by model

Ask ten small-business owners what margin they make and you'll get ten answers — and at least a few of them will actually be quoting markup. The two numbers describe the same profit from different angles, and confusing them can wipe out your planned profit without anyone noticing.

The definitions

Markup compares profit to cost. Margin compares profit to selling price. Same dollars of profit, different denominator.

Markup = (Price − Cost) ÷ Cost
Margin = (Price − Cost) ÷ Price

Example: you buy a lamp for $40 and sell it for $60. Profit is $20. Markup is $20 ÷ $40 = 50%. Margin is $20 ÷ $60 = 33.3%.

The trap

Suppose your business plan needs a 40% gross margin to cover rent, staff and marketing. A new employee prices products at "cost plus 40%". That's a 40% markup, which gives a 28.6% margin. You're 11.4 percentage points short on every sale — and the shortfall shows up only months later in the accounts.

Markup = Margin ÷ (1 − Margin)  ·  Margin = Markup ÷ (1 + Markup)

Get the cost right first

Both numbers are only as good as the cost you plug in. Use landed cost: purchase price plus inbound shipping, duties, packaging, payment processing and an allowance for returns and damage. For services, include the fully-loaded cost of the hours delivered, not just wages.

Which one should your team use?

Standardise on margin for targets and reporting — that's how your accountant, lender and investors think. If your staff price with markup for speed, give them a cheat sheet that converts your target margin into the correct markup multiplier. Or simply link them to the calculator on this site.

Typical gross margins by model

Ranges vary widely, but as rough orientation: software businesses often operate at 70–85% gross margin, specialty retail and DTC brands at 40–60%, grocery at much lower levels, and AI-heavy products somewhere in between depending on model costs. Compare yourself with businesses that share your cost structure, not with the average.
