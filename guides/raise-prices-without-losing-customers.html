How to Raise Prices Without Losing Customers

A step-by-step plan for price increases: the break-even churn math, communication templates and grandfathering strategies.

By 89944 Price Lab Editorial · Updated November 2026

On this page
  1. Step 1: Know your break-even churn
  2. Step 2: Segment before you raise
  3. Step 3: Pair the increase with value
  4. Step 4: Communicate clearly
  5. Step 5: Decide on grandfathering
  6. Step 6: Measure

Most businesses raise prices too rarely and too timidly. Costs creep up every year, but prices stay frozen out of fear that customers will leave. The math usually says you can afford to lose more customers than you think — and in practice, most don't leave at all.

Step 1: Know your break-even churn

If your contribution margin is 50% and you raise prices 10%, your contribution per sale rises from 50 to 60 cents per original dollar. You could lose up to one in six customers (about 16.7%) and still earn the same profit. Few well-handled price rises lose anything close to that.

Break-even volume loss = Price increase ÷ (Contribution margin + Price increase)

Step 2: Segment before you raise

Not every customer is equally price sensitive. Separate new customers from existing ones, and high-usage accounts from casual ones. A common approach is to raise prices for new customers first, watch conversion for a few weeks, then roll the change to existing customers with notice.

Step 3: Pair the increase with value

An increase lands better when it comes with something new: a feature, faster delivery, longer support hours, a better guarantee. Even modest improvements change the story from "we're charging you more" to "we've made it better".

Step 4: Communicate clearly

  • Give advance notice — 30 days is a common standard for subscriptions.
  • Be direct about the new price and the date. Don't bury it.
  • Explain the reason briefly and honestly.
  • Offer an option: lock in the current rate with an annual plan, or move to a lower tier.

Step 5: Decide on grandfathering

Keeping loyal customers on old pricing for a set period (for example, 6–12 months) builds goodwill. Permanent grandfathering, however, creates a growing block of under-priced accounts. Time-limit it.

Step 6: Measure

Track churn, downgrades, conversion rate and revenue per customer for the three months after the change. Compare with the break-even churn you calculated in step 1.
