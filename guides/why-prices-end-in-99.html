Why Prices End in .99: The Psychology Behind Charm Pricing

How the left-digit effect, price-image signals and reference prices make $9.99 outsell $10 — and when round prices win instead.

By 89944 Price Lab Editorial · Updated November 2026

  2. 2. Price image: 9 means "deal"

Walk into almost any store and you will see the same thing: $4.99, $19.99, $899. Prices that end in 9 are so common that most shoppers stop noticing them. That's exactly why they work. This guide explains the three mechanisms behind charm pricing, the evidence for it, and the situations where it backfires.

1. The left-digit effect

We read numbers left to right and we are lazy about it. When you glance at $2.99, your brain encodes "two dollars and something" before it processes the rest. The first digit acts as an anchor, so $2.99 is perceived as much closer to $2 than to $3 — even though it is a penny away from $3.

The effect is strongest when the leftmost digit changes. Going from $3.00 to $2.99 changes the anchor from 3 to 2. Going from $3.60 to $3.59 changes nothing that matters. That's why the calculator on this site flags whether your charm price actually drops the left digit.

2. Price image: 9 means "deal"

Decades of exposure have taught shoppers that prices ending in 9 usually signal a sale or a value-focused retailer. The ending itself carries meaning. A widely cited mail-order experiment found that a women's clothing item sold better at $39 than at either $34 or $44 — the 9-ending beat a lower price. The ending signalled "good deal" more strongly than the actual price difference did.

3. How common is it?

Very. Research reported in the Marketing Bulletin found that roughly 60% of advertised prices end in 9, about 30% end in 5, and only around 7% end in 0. When a pricing convention is that universal, deviating from it is itself a signal.

When round prices win

Round prices — $100, $2,000, $50 — feel complete, confident and premium. They are processed fluently, which people associate with feeling right. Use them when:

  • You sell on quality or status (luxury goods, fine dining, premium services).
  • The purchase is emotional rather than analytical (gifts, experiences).
  • You sell B2B or high-ticket services, where .99 can look like a retail gimmick.
  • You want to simplify mental math, for example in subscription tiers or bundles.

.99 vs .95 vs .97

Within charm pricing, the specific ending also signals something. .99 is the classic everyday price. .95 feels slightly softer and is common in apparel and grocery. .97 and .98 are used by some big-box retailers internally to mark clearance or manager markdowns, and deal hunters know it. Pick an ending and use it consistently so shoppers learn what your prices mean.

Does it still work online?

Yes, with nuance. On comparison sites and marketplaces, shoppers sort by price, so a $19.99 product lists below a $20 product — a practical advantage on top of the psychological one. But when buyers compare on a calculated metric (price per seat, price per kilogram, cost per month) the headline ending matters less than the metric.

How to test it properly

  1. Pick one high-traffic product and two price points (e.g., $49 vs $49.99, or $50 vs $49).
  2. Split traffic randomly, not by day of week.
  3. Measure revenue per visitor, not just conversion rate.
  4. Run until you have statistical confidence — usually at least a few hundred conversions per variant.
Bottom line: charm pricing is a reliable default for value-positioned, impulse and comparison-shopped products. Round pricing is the better choice when your brand sells quality, confidence or simplicity.
