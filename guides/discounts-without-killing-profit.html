How to Run Discounts Without Killing Your Profit

The math of promotions, why 20% off can require 67% more sales, and smarter alternatives like bundles and thresholds.

By 89944 Price Lab Editorial · Updated November 2026

On this page
  1. The math nobody checks
  2. Stacking discounts
  3. Better than a straight discount
  4. Target the people who need a nudge
  5. Pre-promo checklist

Discounts are the easiest growth lever to pull and the hardest to undo. Done well, they clear inventory, win new customers and lift order value. Done badly, they train customers to wait for sales and quietly hand your profit to people who would have paid full price anyway.

The math nobody checks

Say a product sells for $100, costs $50 and earns $50 profit per unit. Run a 20% discount and you earn $30 per unit. To make the same total profit you now need 50 ÷ 30 = 1.67 times the volume — 67% more sales. At 30% off you need double. Very few promotions deliver that lift.

Required sales lift = Old profit per unit ÷ New profit per unit − 1

Stacking discounts

Percentages multiply. 20% off plus an extra 10% is 28%, not 30%. Fixed-dollar coupons hit low-priced items much harder in percentage terms, so set minimum order values on them.

Advertisement

Better than a straight discount

  • Free-shipping thresholds: set the threshold 20–30% above your average order value.
  • Bundles: pair a hero product with a high-margin accessory at a combined price.
  • Gift with purchase: a perceived value of $20 that costs you $4.
  • Tiered offers: "Save $10 on $75, $25 on $150."
  • Loyalty pricing: reward repeat customers rather than discounting to everyone.

Target the people who need a nudge

The most profitable discount is the one only marginal buyers see: first-time visitors, cart abandoners, lapsed customers. Blanket sitewide sales give away margin to your most loyal customers who would have bought anyway.

Pre-promo checklist

  1. What's the goal — new customers, clearance, cash flow, or order value?
  2. What lift is required to break even? (Use the calculator.)
  3. Who sees it, and for how long?
  4. How will you measure incremental sales, not just total sales?
  5. What's the exit plan so it doesn't become your new normal price?
