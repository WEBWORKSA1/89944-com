What is charm pricing?

Charm pricing means setting a price just below a round number: $9.99 instead of $10, $199 instead of $200. The tiny gap in money creates an outsized gap in perception because shoppers read prices left to right and anchor on the first digit. A study summarised in the Marketing Bulletin found roughly six in ten advertised prices end in 9 — retailers use it because it keeps working.

How this calculator decides

  • Classic .99 — the default for mass-market goods and impulse buys.
  • .95 ending — reads slightly friendlier and is common in apparel and groceries.
  • .97 ending — many shoppers associate it with clearance, so use it for markdowns.
  • Left-digit drop — the biggest psychological jump happens when the first digit changes ($100 → $99.99, $2,000 → $1,999.99).
  • Prestige round — whole numbers signal quality; luxury brands, consultants and B2B software often avoid .99 on purpose.

When not to use .99

If you sell on trust and quality — fine dining, premium services, enterprise contracts — a charm ending can make you look cheap. Test round pricing there. Charm prices also matter less when the customer compares on a calculated metric (price per unit, price per seat) instead of the headline price.

Test it, don't guess

Run an A/B test for two to four weeks with enough traffic to reach significance. Track conversion rate and revenue per visitor: a lower price that converts slightly better can still earn less overall.

FAQ

Does $9.99 really sell better than $10?

Often yes for consumer goods, because of the left-digit effect. The lift varies by category and audience, so test it on your own store.

Should SaaS pricing end in 9?

Self-serve tiers often use $9, $19, $49. Sales-led and enterprise plans usually use round numbers to signal value.

What about $8.99 vs $9.99?

Same left digit logic: $8.99 feels like 'eight-something'. Use the tool to compare the margin cost of each step down.
